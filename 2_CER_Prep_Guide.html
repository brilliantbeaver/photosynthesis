1 · What CER is — and why teachers use it

CER stands for Claim, Evidence, Reasoning. It is how scientists argue: they give an answer, prove it with data, and explain the science that connects the two. A CER answer is not a summary of the whole lab. It is a short, focused argument that answers one question.

Why teachers use CER. It shows real understanding: anyone can copy a number, but explaining why it came out that way proves you know the science. It is fair to grade, because each part earns its own points (Section 2). And it is this unit’s big task: [P2 Part VII] asks you to “write a claim, provide evidence (numbers), and a scientific explanation incorporating photosynthesis and respiration. (Color code or label your C, E, and R.)”

→ Tip — Color code AND label

Claim = blue [C] · Evidence = green [E] · Reasoning = orange [R]. Always write the letters too, so your answer still works in pencil, on a black-and-white copy, and for color-blind readers. Every model answer in this guide is labeled this way.

Same question, two answers: 6/6 vs 3/6

Question: In Part III, why did the dissolved oxygen (DO) go down at 20 % light but go up at 100 % light? [P2 Part III]

Part√ 6/6 answer× 3/6 answer
Claim

[C] At 20 % light the plant’s respiration used more O2 than photosynthesis made, so the DO fell; at 100 % light photosynthesis made more O2 than respiration used, so the DO rose.

1/1 answers both halves of the question.

[C] The DO went down at 20 % because there wasn’t much light, and it went up at 100 % because there was more light.

1/1 answers the question in one sentence.

Evidence

[E] At 20 % the DO fell by 0.1928 mg/L per hour, almost the same as in the dark run (−0.1939 mg/L per hour, Part II). At 100 % it rose by 0.4037 mg/L per hour (7.0064 → 7.4101 mg/L).

2/2 specific numbers with units, plus the dark-run baseline.

[E] At 100 % it went up by 0.4037.

1/2 only one number, with no unit (0.4037 what?). Nothing at all for 20 %.

Reasoning

[R] Plants do both processes at once. Respiration in the mitochondria uses O2 all the time; photosynthesis in the chloroplasts uses light energy and releases O2 as a product. The DO difference is the net: O2 made − O2 used. At 20 % photosynthesis added almost nothing (0.0011 mg/L per hour), so the DO fell as in the dark; at 100 % it made 0.5976, more than the 0.1939 used, so the water gained O2.

3/3 both processes, organelles, O2 as a product, and “net = made − used” tied to the numbers.

[R] Plants need light to do photosynthesis, which makes oxygen.

1/3 vague. Photosynthesis making O2 cannot explain why the DO went down: respiration is missing, and nothing links back to the numbers.

To turn the 3/6 into a 6/6: add the 20 % numbers with units, name respiration (uses O2 all the time, in the mitochondria), and explain “DO difference = O2 made − O2 used”.

4 · Writing strong claims

A strong claim answers the exact question, uses the question’s own variables, gives a direction (increases/decreases), and stays inside what was tested. The question’s wording tells you the template:

Question typeClaim templateExample from your packets
Trend — “How does X affect Y?”“As [IV] increases, [DV] increases / decreases.”“As light intensity increases, O2 production increases.” [P2 Part III]
Best condition — “Which is best / optimal?”“[Condition] gives the most [DV].”“100 % light gave the most O2 production of the light levels tested.” [P2 Part III]
Comparison — “Compare A and B.”“[A] produced more [DV] than [B].”“At 100 % light the plant produced more O2 than at 60 % light.” [P2 Part III]
Mechanism — “Why does … happen?”“[Pattern] happens because [process].”“CO2 at Mauna Loa falls each summer because Northern-Hemisphere plants remove more CO2 by photosynthesis than respiration and decay add.” [P3]
Model-based — “What will happen if…?”“[Action] will increase / decrease [quantity].”“Burning fossil fuels will increase the amount of CO2 in the atmosphere.” [P1 Model 2, Q19]

Quick practice: write the claim (then check)

1. Which light intensity gave the most O2 production in Part III? (best condition)

2. Why did the DO go down in the Part II dark run? (mechanism)

3. What would happen to atmospheric CO2 if a large forest were cut down and burned? [P1 Q20] (model-based)

Show answers 1–3
  1. “100 % light gave the most O2 production of the light levels tested (20–100 %).” Not “100 % is the best light for plants”: you only tested up to 100 %.
  2. “The DO went down in the dark because the plant’s cellular respiration used O2 while no photosynthesis was happening.”
  3. “Cutting down and burning a large forest would increase the amount of CO2 in the atmosphere.” (Fewer plants remove CO2, and burning releases the trees’ stored carbon.)

5 · Choosing evidence

Evidence is the specific data that proves your claim. Choose the right numbers, not all of them.

How much evidence is enough?

  • 2–3 data points, with units, that directly support the claim. A change (“from 0.0011 to 0.5976 mg/L per hour”) beats five numbers with no comparison. Describe the pattern in words too: “From 40 % up, each extra 20 % of light added about 0.20 mg/L per hour.”
  • Include the baseline when you explain a change: the Part II dark run (−0.1939 mg/L per hour) shows what respiration alone does.

Data quality: how far can you trust the numbers?

  • Trials and noise: Part II averaged 3 trials (−0.1941, −0.1908, −0.1973 → −0.1939 mg/L per hour), which differ by up to 0.0065, so values smaller than about ±0.005 (like 0.0011 and 0.0002) can’t be told apart from zero. Part III used only 1 trial per light level — a limitation worth naming.
  • Controls: the Part II dark run is the baseline (respiration alone), and everything except light was kept the same (0 fish, 1 plant, 25 °C, white light, 60 minutes), so light caused the change in O2 production.

DO difference vs O2 production — and the sign trap

Your table has two different “oxygen” numbers. The DO difference (= net change, end − start) is what you measured: O2 made − O2 used. O2 production (= gross) is the total O2 made by photosynthesis. The worksheet formula is O2 production = DO difference − respiration. Because respiration is a negative number (−0.1939), subtracting it means adding 0.1939: −(−0.1939) = +0.1939. Quote the right one, and name it: how much O2 the plant made → O2 production (0.5976 at 100 %); what happened to the O2 in the water → DO difference (+0.4037 at 100 %).

6 · Building reasoning

Reasoning is worth the most points (3 of 6). It has two parts: a science principle (a rule that is always true) and a link showing how it explains your evidence. Pick the 1–3 principles that fit from this toolkit.

#The Reasoning Toolkit: principle in one line († = standard biology a little beyond your packets)Use it when…
1Light is the energy source for photosynthesis; its energy is stored as chemical energy in glucose.light intensity or color; “where does the energy come from?”
2O2 is a product of photosynthesis: more photosynthesis → more O2 released.your evidence is DO or O2 production
3CO2 is a reactant of photosynthesis: photosynthesis removes CO2 from the air or water.Keeling Curve; carbon cycle; plant-and-fish tanks
4Respiration uses O2 and releases CO2 all the time (light or dark), in the mitochondria, making ATP.the DO fell; the dark run; fish; CO2 rising in fall
5Plants do both (chloroplasts AND mitochondria). Animals only respire. Decomposers respire too.“Do plants need mitochondria?” [P1 Q5–10]
6Net vs gross: DO difference = O2 made − O2 used; O2 production = total O2 made.why the DO rose or fell; finding O2 production
7 †Chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue/violet light and reflects green. White light contains all colors.light color (Part IV)
8 †Enzymes have an optimum temperature: slower below it, less efficient above it; respiration speeds up when warmer.temperature (Part V); “why not hotter?”
9 †Limiting factors: the rate is held back by whichever factor is in shortest supply (light, CO2, temperature).“Why did it keep rising?” “What else limits it?”
10Carbon cycle balance: without humans, CO2 taken in by photosynthesis ≈ CO2 returned by respiration and decay.Model 2 questions [P1 Q18]
11Fossil fuels release stored carbon: burning moves ancient carbon into the air as CO2; deforestation removes CO2 absorbers.human impact; the long-term rise [P1 Q19–20]
12The CO2 level is a running total (like water in a bathtub): it rises whenever more is added than removed.why CO2 peaks in May; why CO2 keeps rising [P3]
◊ How to think about it

Linking words that make the link visible: this shows that… · this happens because… · since… · therefore… · as a result… · this means… · …which is why… · at the same time… (more in Section 11). The delete test: cover your reasoning. If your answer still says the same thing, the reasoning only repeated the evidence.

7 · Counterclaim and rebuttal (CERR)

When a question gives an opposing idea (“A student says…”) or asks you to argue or evaluate, add a counterclaim (a reasonable opposing idea, such as a watch-out from Section 10) and a rebuttal (evidence + reasoning that show why it is weaker). A good rebuttal can lift a strong answer to an excellent one.

Sentence frames, in order: (1) state the counterclaim fairly: “Some might argue that…” (2) point to evidence against it: “However, the data show…” (3) explain the science: “This is because…” (4) return to your claim: “Therefore…”

▪ Example — Worked rebuttal: “Plants don’t need mitochondria because they make their own food.”

[C] Plants need mitochondria, because plant cells carry out cellular respiration. Some might argue that plants don’t need mitochondria because they make their own food. [E] However, in the Part II dark run (0 % light, 0 fish, 1 plant) the DO fell in all three trials: −0.1941, −0.1908 and −0.1973 mg/L per hour (average −0.1939). [R] With no light there was no photosynthesis, and with no fish the plant was the only organism in the tank. The process that uses up O2 is cellular respiration, which happens in the mitochondria. Making food is not the same as using it: the plant makes glucose by photosynthesis, but its cells still break that glucose down in their mitochondria to release energy as ATP. Therefore, the counterclaim is wrong: plants need both chloroplasts and mitochondria [P1 Q6].

Model answer — Example A (6/6)
CLAIManswers the question

In the range we tested (20–100 %), as light intensity increases, the plant’s O2 production increases.

EVIDENCEspecific data / facts
  • O2 production rose from 0.0011 mg/L per hour at 20 % light to 0.5976 mg/L per hour at 100 % light.
  • From 40 % up, each extra 20 % of light added about 0.20 mg/L per hour (0.0002 → 0.1985 → 0.4023 → 0.5976).
  • The water went from losing O2 (DO difference −0.1928 mg/L per hour at 20 %) to gaining it (+0.4037 at 100 %).
REASONINGthe science "why"

Light is the energy source for photosynthesis (6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6O2). Chlorophyll in the chloroplasts absorbs light energy, so more light means faster photosynthesis and more O2, a product, released. At the same time the plant’s mitochondria use O2 for cellular respiration, light or dark (−0.1939 mg/L per hour in the Part II dark run). At low light respiration used more O2 than photosynthesis made, so the DO fell; at high light photosynthesis made more than respiration used, so the DO rose. Because O2 production was still rising steadily at 100 %, light was the limiting factor over the whole range we tested.

Why it scores 6/6. C (1) exact question, a direction, inside the tested range. E (2) three specific comparisons with units. R (3) energy source, product, organelles, respiration at the same time, every number linked.

! Watch out — Two tempting wrong versions
  • “Higher light means more oxygen because plants need light. At 100 % it was 0.5976.” → 3/6 (C 1 · E 1 · R 1). One number, no unit, no comparison. “Plants need light” has no energy, no product, no respiration.
  • “As light increases, O2 production increases. At 100 % it was 0.4037 and at 40 % it was −0.1937, so at 40 % there was no photosynthesis. 200 % light would be even better.” → 1/6 (C 1 · E 0 · R 0). Quotes the DO difference as O2 production (sign trap). The DO fell because respiration outpaced photosynthesis, not because it stopped. 200 % is beyond the data.

Example B · Why CO2 peaks in May (the Keeling Curve)

Data: your notes [P3] + NOAA / Scripps monthly values · question type: mechanism

Question. The Keeling Curve shows that CO2 at Mauna Loa rises and falls every year. It is highest in May and lowest in September–October. Explain this seasonal pattern using photosynthesis and cellular respiration.

Hint 1 (a nudge)

Which process removes CO2 from the air, and in which season is it strongest? Which processes add CO2 all year?

Hint 2 (a stronger hint)

Think of a bathtub: the level keeps rising as long as more flows in than out. When does “out” (photosynthesis) finally catch up with “in” (respiration + decay)?

Show the think-aloud (steps 1–5)
StepWhat you think (out loud)
1 · Unpack“Explain this pattern” → mechanism claim: “[Pattern] happens because [process].” Use both processes. The “variable” is the season.
2 · Read the dataPeak ≈ 432 ppm (May 2026); low ≈ 426.1 ppm (Sept 2026, preliminary): about 6 ppm lower. Repeats every year; the Southern Hemisphere is opposite and weaker [P3].
3 · ClaimMechanism template. Trap: my notes say “photosynthesis is a delayed reaction” (watch-out 8). The real reason is the running total.
4 · EvidenceThe two monthly values in ppm, the size of the swing, and the timing (high in May, low in Sept/Oct).
5 · ReasoningToolkit #3 (CO2 is a reactant), #4 (respiration and decay all year), #12 (running total). Explain the fall, the rise, and why the turn comes in May.
Show the model answer (Example B)
Model answer — Example B (6/6)
CLAIManswers the question

CO2 at Mauna Loa peaks in May and is lowest in September–October because Northern-Hemisphere plants remove CO2 by photosynthesis mainly in spring and summer, while respiration and decay add CO2 all year.

EVIDENCEspecific data / facts
  • CO2 peaked at about 432 ppm in May 2026 (a record) and fell to about 426.1 ppm by September 2026 (NOAA, preliminary): a drop of about 6 ppm in one growing season.
  • The pattern repeats every year, and the Southern Hemisphere, with less land and fewer land plants, shows the opposite, weaker pattern [P3].
REASONINGthe science "why"

Photosynthesis uses CO2 as a reactant (6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6O2). From May to September, trees in full leaf remove more CO2 than respiration and decay add, so the level falls. In fall the leaves drop and photosynthesis slows, but cellular respiration by plants, animals and especially decomposers breaking down fallen leaves keeps releasing CO2, so from October to May more CO2 is added than removed. The CO2 level is a running total, like water in a bathtub: it keeps rising while more flows in than out, peaks in May when photosynthesis catches up, and is lowest in Sept/Oct when photosynthesis drops back below respiration. Photosynthesis is not “delayed”; the level just takes months to build up and draw down.

Why it scores 6/6. C (1) pattern + cause. E (2) two real values with units and dates, the swing, the hemispheres. R (3) CO2 as a reactant, respiration and decay all year, and the running total that explains the timing — the hardest part.

! Watch out — Two tempting wrong versions
  • “CO2 is higher in spring because photosynthesis is a delayed reaction: it takes time to remove CO2.” → 0/6 (C 0 · E 0 · R 0). Keeps a mistaken note (watch-out 8): photosynthesis starts within seconds of light. No numbers, no respiration.
  • “CO2 goes up and down because people burn more fossil fuels in winter. Respiration is constant, so it has a negligible effect.” → 0/6 (C 0 · E 0 · R 0). Fossil fuels explain the long-term rise, not the yearly zig-zag. Respiration plus decay release about as much CO2 each year as photosynthesis removes (watch-out 8).

Show the model answer (Example C, paragraph style)

[C] The optimal conditions were 100 % light intensity, white light, and about 20 °C (the best temperature we tested), and under these conditions one plant could support about 4 fish. [E] In Part III, O2 production rose from 0.0011 mg/L per hour at 20 % light to 0.5976 mg/L per hour at 100 %. The class results showed white light gave the most O2 of the colors tested and 20 °C was best of the five temperatures tested (15–35 °C) [add your class’s Part IV and V numbers here]. One plant at 100 % white light (25 °C, Part III) had a DO difference of +0.4037 mg/L per hour, an O2 surplus. Using HYPOTHETICAL PRACTICE DATA of 0.10 mg/L per hour per fish, 4 fish use 0.40 (DO difference +0.0037, still ≥ 0), but 5 fish use 0.50 (DO difference −0.0963, so the O2 falls). [R] Photosynthesis in the chloroplasts uses light energy, CO2 and H2O to make glucose and releases O2 as a product. Bright light supplies the most energy, white light contains the red and blue light that chlorophyll absorbs best, and the enzymes of photosynthesis work best near their optimum temperature. Cellular respiration in the mitochondria of the plant and the fish uses O2 all the time, and fish only respire. The tank stays healthy only while O2 made ≥ O2 used (DO difference ≥ 0), so the fish’s total respiration must fit inside the plant’s surplus of about 0.40 mg/L per hour: 4 fish fit, 5 do not. The fish also release CO2, a reactant the plant needs, so plant and fish depend on each other.

Why it scores 6/6. C (1) both parts (conditions + fish). E (2) real numbers with units, class results, and labeled practice numbers. R (3) both processes, organelles, reactants/products, and the rule “O2 made ≥ O2 used” that turns numbers into a fish count.

→ Reasonable inference (beyond the packets) — Level-4 thinking

The +0.4037 surplus was measured at 25 °C; at the best tested 20 °C it is probably a little larger, so rerun to check. At night there is no photosynthesis, so no surplus, and the DO falls. And 4 fish leave a margin of only 0.0037 mg/L per hour (about the size of the trial-to-trial spread), so a careful scientist might choose fewer fish.

! Watch out — Two tempting wrong versions
  • “The best conditions are 100 % light, white light and 20 °C because plants need light. The tank could hold lots of fish.” → 1/6 (C 0 · E 0 · R 1). No numbers. “Lots of fish” does not answer “how many”. No respiration, though the question asks for it.
  • “20 °C is best because above 20 °C the enzymes denature. More fish is better because fish make CO2, so 10 fish would make the plant grow fastest.” → 0/6 (C 0 · E 0 · R 0). Enzymes don’t denature just above 20 °C (watch-out 10). It ignores that fish use O2: with the practice data, 10 fish would use 1.0 mg/L per hour, over twice the surplus.

9 · Upgrade a weak answer

Use the rubric like a repair manual: check the claim, then the evidence, then the reasoning, and fix the weakest part first. Try each upgrade before you open the answer.

Weak answer 1 — your own Part III conclusion [P2 Part III Q8]

“Higher light intensity generally means higher oxygen production.” (About 1/6 as a CER: a good claim, but no evidence and no reasoning.)

  • Fix C: keep it (it has a direction); say “O2 production” and swap “generally” for the tested range.
  • Fix E: add a comparison with units: 0.0011 → 0.5976 mg/L per hour; DO difference −0.1928 → +0.4037.
  • Fix R: light = energy source; O2 = product; respiration uses O2 at the same time.
Show the upgraded answer 1

[C] In the range we tested (20–100 %), higher light intensity means higher O2 production. [E] O2 production rose from 0.0011 mg/L per hour at 20 % light to 0.5976 mg/L per hour at 100 %, and the DO difference changed from −0.1928 to +0.4037 mg/L per hour. [R] Light is the energy source for photosynthesis, so more light lets the chloroplasts make glucose faster and release more O2, a product. The plant’s mitochondria use O2 for respiration all the time, so at low light the DO fell; at high light photosynthesis made more O2 than respiration used, so the DO rose.

Weak answer 2 — the temperature reason [P2 Part VI]

“20 °C is best because at higher temperatures the enzymes are denatured.” (About 1/6: a claim with no numbers and a science error.)

  • Fix C: say “best tested”: only 15–35 °C in 5 °C steps were tested, so the true optimum is between 15 and 25 °C.
  • Fix E: get the class’s Part V numbers: O2 production at 20 °C vs a cooler and a warmer temperature, with units.
  • Fix R: above the optimum, enzymes work less efficiently (real denaturing needs roughly 40 °C+), and respiration speeds up.
Show the upgraded answer 2

[C] Of the temperatures we tested (15–35 °C), 20 °C gave the most O2 production. [E] At 20 °C, O2 production was ___ mg/L per hour, compared with ___ mg/L per hour at 15 °C and ___ mg/L per hour at 30 °C [your class’s Part V data]. [R] Photosynthesis is run by enzymes, which work fastest near an optimum temperature. Below it, molecules move and collide more slowly, so the reactions slow down. Above it, the enzymes work less efficiently (real denaturing needs roughly 40 °C or more), and the plant’s respiration speeds up and uses more O2, so the water gains less O2.

Weak answer 3 — burning fossil fuels [P1 Model 2, Q19]

Question: How does burning fossil fuels affect the carbon cycle? Weak answer: “It makes pollution and is bad for the environment.” (0/6: true-ish, but it does not answer the question.)

  • Fix C: use the model-based template: “[Action] will increase / decrease [quantity].”
  • Fix E: Model 2’s combustion arrow + real data: about 313 ppm (1958) → about 432 ppm (May 2026); about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution.
  • Fix R: natural balance [P1 Q18] + stored carbon released faster than photosynthesis removes it + running total.
Show the upgraded answer 3

[C] Burning fossil fuels increases the amount of CO2 in the atmosphere and upsets the balance of the carbon cycle. [E] In Model 2, combustion moves carbon from carbon stores (coal, oil, natural gas) into atmospheric CO2 [P1 Model 2]. At Mauna Loa, CO2 rose from about 313 ppm in 1958 to about 432 ppm in May 2026, far above the pre-industrial level of about 280 ppm (Scripps / NOAA). [R] Without humans, the CO2 taken in by photosynthesis roughly equals the CO2 returned by respiration and decay, so the level stays about constant [P1 Q18]. Burning fossil fuels adds carbon stored for millions of years to the air as CO2, faster than photosynthesis can remove it. Because the CO2 level is a running total, adding more than is removed each year makes it keep rising (recently about 2–2.5 ppm per year).

10 · Common mistakes (and one-line fixes)

#Watch-outOne-line fix
1Swapping the energy labels (light in for photosynthesis, ATP out for respiration)Photosynthesis takes in light energy; respiration releases energy as ATP.
2Forgetting plants also respirePlants have chloroplasts AND mitochondria; they respire 24/7, light or dark.
3Saying animals lack mitochondriaAnimals have mitochondria; what they lack is chloroplasts.
4Mixing up DO difference (net) and O2 production (gross) — and the minus-a-negative sign trapO2 production = DO difference − (−0.1939) = DO difference + 0.1939.
5Evidence without numbers/unitsEvery evidence sentence gets a number + unit (mg/L per hour, %, °C, ppm).
6Reasoning that just repeats the evidenceAdd a principle (“because light is the energy source…”) and link it.
7Claiming beyond the data (e.g., “200 % light would be even better”)Say “in the range we tested (20–100 %)”.
8Keeping the mistaken notes (“respiration is negligible”, “photosynthesis is delayed”)Respiration + decay release about as much CO2 as photosynthesis removes; the level is a running total.
9“Respiration = breathing”Breathing moves gases; cellular respiration is a chemical reaction in mitochondria.
10Saying enzymes “denature” just above 20 °CAbove the optimum they work less efficiently; denaturing needs roughly 40 °C+.
11Graph not matching the table / uneven scale / drawing beyond the dataIV on x, DV on y, even ticks, mark every point, stop at the last point.
12Forgetting units and the time basis (per hour vs per minute)Write “mg/L per hour” or “mg/L per minute”; per minute = per hour ÷ 60.
