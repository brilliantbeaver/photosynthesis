● Key idea — The 60-second version
  • Photosynthesis (chloroplasts): 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6O2. Light energy is stored as chemical energy in glucose.
  • Cellular respiration (mitochondria; plants AND animals; 24/7): C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy (ATP).

Part 1 · The big picture: energy flows, matter cycles

Every living thing needs energy and matter (atoms to build its body). Producers (plants, algae) make their own food by photosynthesis. Consumers (animals) eat other living things. Decomposers (fungi, bacteria, worms) break down dead things and wastes, returning matter to the air and soil.

● Key idea — Matter cycles; energy flows

Matter (CO2, H2O, glucose, O2) is recycled: the same atoms go round and round [P1 Q3].

Energy is not recycled: it enters as light, is stored in food, is used as ATP and leaves as heat — so sunlight must be constantly available [P1 Q4].

Part 2 · Photosynthesis up close

Photosynthesis is the process in which producers use light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into glucose (a sugar) and oxygen. It happens in chloroplasts, where the green pigment chlorophyll captures the light.

→ Reasonable inference (beyond the packets) — Where the inputs come from

CO2 enters the leaf through tiny pores called stomata, and O2 leaves the same way. Water comes up from the roots through the stem and leaf veins. (For your lab’s aquatic plant, CO2 and O2 are dissolved in the water.)

Zooming in: leaf → cell → chloroplast → chlorophyll

The energy change: light → chemical

Photosynthesis stores energy: light energy becomes chemical energy in the bonds of glucose. The plant uses some glucose for its own respiration, stores some as starch (seeds, roots, fruit) and builds new cells with the rest.

→ Reasonable inference (beyond the packets) — Enrichment: where does the O₂ come from?

From splitting water (H2O) with light energy — not from CO2, whose carbon ends up in glucose.

Part 3 · Cellular respiration up close

Cellular respiration is the process in which cells break down glucose using oxygen to release its stored energy as ATP; carbon dioxide and water are released. It happens in the mitochondria of plant AND animal cells.

ATP — the cell’s energy currency. Glucose holds a lot of energy, but cells can’t “spend” it directly. Cellular respiration transfers its energy into many small, usable packets called ATP — like changing a big bill into coins.

Every job a cell does is paid for with ATP: muscles contracting, building new cells, moving materials, sending nerve signals — and, in plants, growing. Some energy is always lost as heat along the way.

Figure 5. Respiration turns stored energy (glucose) into spendable energy (ATP).

Respiration ≠ breathing

! Watch out — Two classic mistakes
  • “Respiration = breathing.” Breathing moves gases in and out of the lungs; cellular respiration is a chemical reaction in the mitochondria of cells.
  • Forgetting that plants respire. Plants respire 24/7; photosynthesis needs light. By day a plant usually makes more O2 than it uses; at night it only respires, using O2 and releasing CO2.

DO difference vs. O₂ production: the oxygen bank account

With the light on, the plant does two things at once: photosynthesis adds O2 (a deposit) and respiration uses O2 (a withdrawal). The DO reading only shows the change in the balance.

● Key idea — Two different numbers — use the packet words
  • DO difference (net change) = DO end − DO start = O2 made − O2 used. In the dark it is just respiration (−0.1939).
  • O2 production (gross) = all the O2 made by photosynthesis = DO difference + 0.1939.
  • Sign trap: the sheet says DO difference − respiration, and respiration is negative: 0.4037 − (−0.1939) = 0.4037 + 0.1939 = 0.5976. Mind the time basis too: per hour (60-minute run); per minute = ÷ 60.

REASONINGthe science “why”
Light is the energy source for photosynthesis. More light means chlorophyll in the chloroplasts absorbs more light energy, so photosynthesis runs faster and releases more O2, one of its products. Cellular respiration uses O2 all the time, so in dim light (20–40 %) it used O2 much faster than photosynthesis made it, and the DO fell by about as much as in the dark. Because O2 production never leveled off, light was the limiting factor from 40 % to 100 %.

Part 6 · What controls the rate: light, color, temperature, CO₂

A limiting factor is whatever is in shortest supply and holds the rate back — like the narrowest part of a pipe. The classic three for photosynthesis are light intensity, temperature and CO2 concentration; light color matters too.

Light intensity (your team, Part III): more light → more light energy absorbed by chlorophyll → faster photosynthesis → more O2. Your O2 production stayed near zero at 20–40 %, then rose steadily from 40 % to 100 % with no leveling off, so light was the limiting factor from 40 % to 100 %. With even more light, another factor would eventually limit it (inference). Class best: 100 % [P2 Part VI].

Figure 13. The limiting-factor idea: the narrowest section sets the flow.

→ Reasonable inference (beyond the packets) — CO₂ and going further

CO2 is a reactant: more of it (e.g., baking-soda water) speeds up photosynthesis until another factor limits it — then the curve levels off. Other ways to measure the rate: count the O2 bubbles from Elodea, or time how fast leaf disks float up. Where does a plant’s mass come from? Mostly from CO2 in the air built into glucose, not from the soil.

Light color (Part IV, another team)

Class result: white light gave the most O2 [P2 Part VI]. Your reason (“white contains the most colors”) is on the right track — sharpen it:

  • Chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue/violet light and reflects green — that is why leaves look green.
  • White light contains red and blue (and every other color), so the plant gets more light energy it can use.
  • Among single colors, red and blue should work best and green worst.

Temperature (Part V, another team)

The class’s best tested temperature was 20 °C [P2 Part VI]. Only 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35 °C were tested, so the true optimum is somewhere between 15 and 25 °C. Say “the class’s results showed…”, not “plants are always best at 20 °C”.

Too cold: molecules move and collide more slowly, so the enzyme-driven reactions slow down. Warmer than the optimum: O2 production falls because enzymes work less efficiently; respiration also speeds up and uses more O2, so the net gain (the DO difference) falls even faster.

√ Fix-it from your worksheet — “At higher temps (>20 °C), enzymes are denatured” [P2 Part VI]

Your cold-side reason is √ correct. But enzymes do not denature just above 20 °C — above the optimum they simply work less efficiently while respiration speeds up. Real denaturing of most plant enzymes needs about 40 °C or more.

What is productivity? [P2 Part VI Q2]

√ Fix-it from your worksheet — Unanswered on your sheet — here is the idea

Productivity = the rate at which producers make organic compounds (food such as glucose); the sheet’s “release” wording is garbled. Why DO? O2 is released in a fixed ratio as glucose is made (6 O2 per glucose) and is easy to measure in water, so DO is a stand-in (proxy) for how fast food is made. More than oxygen? Yes — it really means making organic compounds (glucose → starch → new plant material); O2 is just the easiest product to measure. The DO difference measures net productivity (after the plant’s own respiration).

Putting it together: the optimal ecosystem [P2 Part VII]

Part VII — the model for your CER test — asks for the best conditions and how many fish the tank can support. Class results: 100 % light, white light, about 20 °C (best tested). The logic: fish only respire (use O2, release CO2). The tank stays healthy only while the DO difference stays ≥ 0. One plant at 100 % white light and 25 °C gave +0.4037 mg/L per hour — that surplus is the O2 “budget” for the fish.

  • Example — HYPOTHETICAL PRACTICE DATA: if one fish used 0.10 mg/L per hour, the tank could support 4 fish (4 × 0.10 = 0.40 ≤ 0.4037, leaving +0.0037: a very thin margin — inside the trial-to-trial spread) but not 5 (0.50 > 0.4037). Find the real number: the +0.4037 was measured at 25 °C, so first re-measure the plant-only run at your chosen conditions; then add fish one at a time, rerun, and keep the last number with DO difference ≥ 0. More plants or better conditions raise the budget.
  • Interdependence [P1 Q10]: the fish supply CO2 for the plant’s photosynthesis; the plant supplies O2 for the fish.
  • Night check (inference): at night there is no surplus — everything only respires. Over 24 hours (12 h light, 12 h dark) with the HYPOTHETICAL 0.10 mg/L per hour per fish (n = number of fish): 12(0.4037 − 0.10n) − 12(0.1939 + 0.10n) ≥ 0 → n ≤ 1. So a real tank supports far fewer fish unless you add plants.

Part 8 · Humans & the carbon cycle

Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) hold carbon stored underground for millions of years. In about 200 years, humans have burned carbon that took millions of years to store, adding CO2 far faster than nature can absorb it [P1 Q19].

Deforestation has two effects [P1 Q20]: fewer trees remove CO2, and burning or rotting trees release their stored carbon.

The bathtub idea: CO2 in the air is like water in a tub. It rises whenever more flows in (respiration + combustion) than drains out (photosynthesis).

Figure 19. The bathtub model of atmospheric CO₂.

Part 9 · Mauna Loa & the Keeling Curve

Mauna Loa is a still-active volcano in Hawaii; its observatory, about 3,400 m high, samples large, well-mixed air masses [P3]. Charles David Keeling began measuring CO2 there in March 1958 at about 313 ppm (Scripps first reading; ppm = parts per million: CO2 molecules per million air molecules); the graph of his data is the Keeling Curve. May 2026: record peak, about 432 ppm (Scripps; NOAA). Sept 2026: about 426 ppm (NOAA, preliminary). Pre-industrial (before about 1800): about 280 ppm (ice cores).

Why the yearly zig-zag? The running total

The CO2 level is a running total — like water in a bathtub. In fall and winter, leaves drop and photosynthesis slows, but respiration and decay (microbes breaking down fallen leaves) keep adding CO2, so the level keeps rising until it peaks in May, just as leaves come back. Months of strong summer photosynthesis then pull it down to the low in Sept/Oct. The swing comes mostly from the huge forests of the Northern Hemisphere. The Southern Hemisphere has the opposite pattern, and a weaker one, because it has less land and so fewer land plants [P3].

Two fix-its from your Mauna Loa notes [P3 p.1]

√ Fix-it from your worksheet — “Photosynthesis is a delayed reaction”

Partly right: the observation is correct (more CO2 in spring than fall; peak in May, low in Sept/Oct), and the level does lag behind the seasons. But photosynthesis itself starts within seconds of light. The lag happens because the level is a running total: it keeps climbing until photosynthesis removes more than respiration and decay add.

√ Fix-it from your worksheet — “Cellular respiration… has a negligible effect on CO₂”

Likely a note-taking slip. Respiration (plants, animals and especially decomposers) releases a huge amount of CO2 — about as much as photosynthesis removes each year (the balance in your own [P1 Q18]) — and it pushes CO2 up every fall and winter. The lesson probably meant: respiration goes on all year, so the big season-to-season change comes mostly from photosynthesis switching up and down.

§ Where this comes from — Other notes, polished [P3]

  • CO2 began rising with the Industrial Revolution (1800s), from a pre-industrial level (before about 1800) of about 280 ppm; the rise sped up sharply after WWII.
  • √ Right in your notes: extra CO2 from burning fossil fuels builds up faster than Earth can absorb it, and more CO2 traps more heat. (Deforestation is a second human source [P1 Q20].)
