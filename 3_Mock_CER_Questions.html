How to use this set

These 12 questions climb like a staircase, from warm-ups to test-level challenges. Every question is built on the packets you worked through in class: the POGIL worksheet [P1], the simulation lab [P2] and your Mauna Loa notes [P3]. Many use your own lab numbers.

LevelQuestionsWhat it practices
Level 1 · Warm-upQ1–Q3One data set or one model; a clear claim; the basic science of both processes.
Level 2 · Data analysisQ4–Q6Tables and graphs: trends, best conditions, units, and DO difference vs. O2 production.
Level 3 · Scenario / transferQ7–Q9New situations: the Keeling Curve, a sealed-tube experiment, a cleared forest.
Level 4 · ChallengeQ10–Q12Multi-step answers: an oxygen budget, a counterclaim and rebuttal, and upgrading a weak answer.
→ Tip — Real data vs. practice data

Numbers from your packets or from NOAA always say where they come from. Numbers made up for practice are always marked HYPOTHETICAL PRACTICE DATA, both in the question and inside the figure. Other teams did Part IV (light color) and Part V (temperature): before the test, ask them for their real numbers and redo Q5 and Q6 with them.

In the interactive (HTML) version, hints and answers stay hidden until you click. On paper they are printed, so cover them with a sheet of paper while you work.

The scoring rubric (/6)

PartPointsFull pointsPartial points
[C] Claim0–1Accurate, complete, answers the question in one sentence (no "I think").0 = missing, wrong, or does not answer the question.
[E] Evidence0–2At least 2 specific, accurate data points with units that support the claim (a comparison is best).1 = vague ("it went up") or only one data point.
[R] Reasoning0–3Correct science principle(s) + an explicit link from the evidence to the claim + correct vocabulary.2 = principle but weak link; 1 = repeats the evidence or is vague; 0 = wrong science.

Your teacher's rubric may differ slightly, but the habits that earn points are the same.

Label and color your C, E and R

Your lab's CER prompt says "Color code or label your C, E, and R" [P2 Part VII]. Do both: the letter labels still work in black-and-white printing and for color-blind readers. In this booklet: [C] Claim = blue · [E] Evidence = green · [R] Reasoning = orange. A short example:

▪ Example

[C] In the light, the water plant added oxygen to the water. [E] At 100 % light the DO rose from 7.0064 to 7.4101 mg/L in 60 minutes (+0.4037 mg/L per hour). [R] O2 is a product of photosynthesis, which needs light energy; in bright light the plant's photosynthesis made more O2 than its respiration used, so the dissolved O2 went up.

The 5-step CER method (used in every answer key)

  1. Unpack the question: circle the variables (what changed? what was measured?) and the command word.
  2. Read the data: find the trend, the highest and lowest, and 2–3 numbers with units; note anything odd.
  3. Claim it: one sentence that answers the question in its own words.
  4. Prove it (Evidence): 2–3 specific data points with units, ideally a comparison ("from __ to __").
  5. Explain it (Reasoning): the science "because": reactants, products, organelle, energy, and respiration happening at the same time.

Answer key · Q1: Do plants do cellular respiration?

Show the answer key for Q1
CLAIManswers the question
Yes. Plants do cellular respiration: in the dark, the plant used up dissolved oxygen.
EVIDENCEspecific data / facts
  • With 0 % light, 1 plant and 0 fish, the DO fell in all 3 trials (e.g., Trial 1: 7.0735 → 6.8794 mg/L).
  • The average DO difference was −0.1939 mg/L per hour; the trials agree within 0.0065 mg/L.
REASONINGthe science "why"

Cellular respiration uses O2 (C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + ATP) in the mitochondria, which plant cells have along with chloroplasts. With 0 % light there was no photosynthesis to add O2, and with 0 fish the plant was the only living thing, so the steady DO drop must be the plant's own respiration, turning its glucose into ATP. This dark run is the control that measures respiration alone.

◊ How to think about it
  1. Unpack the question: yes/no; a control run.
  2. Read the data: all 3 trials fell about 0.19 mg/L.
  3. Claim it: "Yes, plants do respire."
  4. Prove it (Evidence): one trial + the average.
  5. Explain it (Reasoning): O2 used; rule out light, fish.
! Watch out
  • Forgetting plants respire (watch-out 2).
  • Respiration = breathing (watch-out 9).
  • No units (watch-out 5).

Answer key · Q2: Which arrows are cellular respiration?

Show the answer key for Q2
CLAIManswers the question
A, B and C release CO2 by cellular respiration: A by decomposers (fungi, bacteria, worms), B by animals (the rabbit) and C by plants. D is photosynthesis, which removes CO2.
EVIDENCEspecific data / facts
  • Arrows B (from the rabbit), C (from the plant) and A (from Decay) all point into Atmospheric CO2.
  • Arrow D is the only lettered arrow going the other way: from Atmospheric CO2 into the plant.
REASONINGthe science "why"

CO2 is a product of respiration (C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + ATP), so respiration arrows carry CO2 into the air. Animals, plants and decomposers all break down food for energy (decomposers break down dead matter and wastes), so B, C and A are all respiration. CO2 is a reactant of photosynthesis, so arrow D carries it out of the air into the plant. Combustion also adds CO2, but it is burning outside living cells, not respiration.

◊ How to think about it
  1. Unpack the question: which letters? which organisms?
  2. Read the data: follow each arrow tail → head.
  3. Claim it: "A, B, C = respiration."
  4. Prove it (Evidence): describe 2–3 arrows.
  5. Explain it (Reasoning): CO2 = respiration product.
! Watch out
  • Forgetting plants respire (watch-out 2): arrow C.
  • Missing B or A: animals, decomposers.
  • Combustion is not respiration.

Answer key · Q3: Where does an athlete's energy come from?

Show the answer key for Q3
CLAIManswers the question
The athlete's energy comes from sunlight: the corn captured it by photosynthesis, it passed along the food chain as chemical energy in food, and the athlete's cells released it as ATP by cellular respiration.
EVIDENCEspecific data / facts
  • The chain is sun → corn plant (seeds) → chicken → athlete; the only energy input is sunlight.
  • Model 1: photosynthesis takes in light energy and makes glucose; respiration uses glucose and releases energy as ATP.
REASONINGthe science "why"

In the corn's chloroplasts, photosynthesis turns light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose (kept as starch in the seeds). The chicken eats the seeds and stores some of that energy in its body. In the athlete's muscle cells, cellular respiration in the mitochondria breaks down glucose with O2 and releases the energy as ATP, which powers the muscles. Energy flows one way and some is lost as heat at each step; it is not recycled [P1 Q4], so sunlight must keep coming.

◊ How to think about it
  1. Unpack the question: where from AND how?
  2. Read the data: sun → corn → chicken → athlete.
  3. Claim it: "From sunlight, via food."
  4. Prove it (Evidence): the chain + both equations.
  5. Explain it (Reasoning): light → glucose → ATP.
! Watch out
  • Swapped energy labels (watch-out 1).
  • Missing links: glucose and ATP.
  • "Energy is recycled": it flows.

Answer key · Q6: What temperature is best?

Show the answer key for Q6
CLAIManswers the question
Of the temperatures tested, 20 °C gave the most O2 production; it was lower at both colder and warmer temperatures.
EVIDENCEspecific data / facts
  • O2 production peaked at 0.66 mg/L per hour at 20 °C, vs. 0.40 at 15 °C and 0.28 at 35 °C (HYPOTHETICAL PRACTICE DATA).
  • Respiration rose from 0.11 to 0.32 mg/L per hour (15 → 35 °C); at 35 °C the light DO difference was −0.04.
REASONINGthe science "why"

Photosynthesis is run by enzymes, which have an optimum temperature. At 15 °C molecules move and collide less, so it is slower; above the optimum the enzymes work less efficiently as they start to change shape, so O2 production falls again. Respiration also speeds up with warmth (0.11 → 0.32 mg/L per hour), so the DO difference (net) falls even faster: +0.51 mg/L per hour at 20 °C but −0.04 mg/L per hour at 35 °C, when respiration used more than photosynthesis made. Only 5-degree steps were tested, so the true optimum lies somewhere between 15 and 25 °C.

  2. Read the data: peak at 20 °C; respiration rises.

  5. Explain it (Reasoning): enzyme optimum + respiration.
! Watch out

Answer key · Q7: The Keeling Curve's yearly zig-zag

Show the answer key for Q7
CLAIManswers the question
CO2 falls from May to September because Northern-Hemisphere plants photosynthesize strongly while in leaf, and rises from fall to May because respiration and decay keep adding CO2 while photosynthesis slows. September 2026 is higher than September 2025 because burning fossil fuels adds more CO2 each year than the cycle removes.
EVIDENCEspecific data / facts
  • CO2 rose from 424.4 ppm (Sept 2025) to 432.3 ppm (May 2026), +7.9 ppm, then fell to 426.1 ppm (Sept 2026), −6.2 ppm in 4 months.
  • September 2026 was 1.7 ppm higher than September 2025.
REASONINGthe science "why"

Photosynthesis removes CO2 from the air; respiration by plants, animals and especially decomposers adds it all year. Most land plants are in the Northern Hemisphere: in spring and summer, trees in full leaf remove more CO2 than respiration adds, so the level falls until September/October. In fall and winter, photosynthesis slows but respiration and the decay of fallen leaves continue, so CO2 builds up (like water in a bathtub) until May. On top of this, burning fossil fuels adds carbon stored for millions of years faster than nature removes it, so each low is higher than the last.

◊ How to think about it
  1. Unpack the question: two parts: zig-zag + rise.
  2. Read the data: 424.4 → 432.3 → 426.1 ppm.
  3. Claim it: one clause per part.
  4. Prove it (Evidence): rise, drop, Sept vs. Sept.
  5. Explain it (Reasoning): photosynthesis vs. decay.
! Watch out
  • "Photosynthesis is delayed" (watch-out 8).
  • "Respiration is negligible" (watch-out 8).
  • Units: ppm, not mg/L.

Answer key · Q9: Clearing and burning a forest

Show the answer key for Q9
CLAIManswers the question
Clearing and burning the forest and burning more coal will increase atmospheric CO2, and make it rise faster.
EVIDENCEspecific data / facts
  • In Model 2, only arrow D (photosynthesis, atmosphere → plants) removes CO2; the combustion arrow carries carbon from carbon stores into Atmospheric CO2.
  • The real record: about 280 ppm before the 1800s, 313 ppm in 1958, about 427 ppm in 2025.
REASONINGthe science "why"

Without humans, CO2 taken in by photosynthesis ≈ CO2 returned by respiration, so the level stays about constant [P1 Q18]. Fewer trees means less photosynthesis, so less CO2 is removed. Burning the trees and the coal (combustion) quickly releases carbon stored for decades in wood and for millions of years in coal, and decomposers breaking down leftover wood add more by respiration. More in and less out upsets the balance, so CO2 rises, as the real record shows.

  5. Explain it (Reasoning): less out + more in.
! Watch out
  • "New trees will fix it": decades.
  • Only one side: removal AND release.
  • Fuels store carbon, not CO2.

Show the answer key for Q10
CLAIManswers the question
The best ecosystem uses 100 % white light at about 20 °C (best tested), and one plant could support about 4 fish in the light; at night the tank would lose oxygen, so a real tank needs fewer fish or more plants.

Answer key · Q11: Is respiration "negligible"?

Show the answer key for Q11
Model CERR answer: claim, evidence, reasoning, then counterclaim + rebuttal
CLAIManswers the question
I disagree: cellular respiration has a large effect on atmospheric CO2. It returns about as much CO2 as photosynthesis removes, and it drives the rise in CO2 every fall and winter.
EVIDENCEspecific data / facts
  • A: one plant used 0.1939 mg/L of O2 per hour in the dark (all 3 trials), releasing CO2 as it did. B: three of Model 2's four lettered arrows (A, B, C) are respiration, and [P1 Q18] says plant uptake ≈ respiration.
  • C: CO2 rose 7.9 ppm from September 2025 (424.4) to May 2026 (432.3); the yearly rise was only 1.7 ppm.
REASONINGthe science "why"

Respiration happens in every plant, animal and decomposer, all day and night, so it releases about as much CO2 as photosynthesis removes; that is why nature stays balanced [P1 Q18]. When Northern-Hemisphere photosynthesis slows in fall and winter, respiration and decay keep going and CO2 climbs 7.9 ppm in eight months, more than four times the 1.7 ppm the level gained over the whole year.

[Counterclaim] Some might argue respiration can't matter because it is constant. [Rebuttal] However, constant does not mean small. The season-to-season change does come mostly from photosynthesis switching up and down, but if respiration stopped, the fall-and-winter rise in Source C would disappear. So its effect is huge, not negligible.

  1. Unpack the question: evaluate + CERR.
  2. Read the data: one fact from A, B and C.
  3. Claim it: disagree + the reason.
  4. Prove it (Evidence): numbers from two sources.
  5. Explain it (Reasoning): flow in ≈ out; rebut fairly.
! Watch out
  • "Negligible" is a note slip (watch-out 8).
  • Rebuttal = evidence, not a repeat.
  • Be fair to the counterclaim.
